How I Learned to Say “No” Without Feeling Guilty

Stories · 2026-10-31

I said yes before Theo finished his sentence.

I was in the school library with five heavy books in my arms, already late for something, and he came over holding a rolled-up map of the whole library. “Could you redo the Autumn Reading display this weekend?” he asked. “Every shelf. It’s a lot, but you’re so good at this.”

My mouth said, “Sure, no problem.” My stomach said something much less polite.

I already had a history project, a cousin’s birthday on Saturday, and a promise to myself that I would sleep like a normal human on Sunday. But Theo smiled, and I felt a small cloud of guilt rise above my head at the very idea of disappointing him. Saying yes felt like paying a bill. Saying no felt like stealing.

That night, Zoe came over with a bag of cookies and took one look at my face.

“You said yes to something you didn’t want to do,” she said.

“How do you always know?”

“Your eyebrows,” said Zoe. “They go up and sad.”

· · ·

The kind no

We sat cross-legged on my bed with tea and a floral pillow between us. Zoe took a yellow sticky note, wrote on it in her round handwriting, and pressed it onto the pillow.

Thank you for asking. I can’t this time.

“That’s a whole no,” she said. “It has a thank-you in front, so it’s polite, and a full stop at the end, so it’s finished. No because, no essay, no tiny apology to the door.”

“But what if he’s upset?”

“Then he’s a person who has feelings about a no, and he’s allowed to. You’re allowed to say it anyway. Guilt is just a smoke alarm, Lina. Sometimes it goes off because of burnt toast, not a fire.”

We practiced. I said it to the pillow, and my voice squeaked on “can’t.” I said it to the lamp. I said it in a dramatic movie-trailer voice, and Zoe laughed so hard she fell sideways into the blankets with a cookie in her hand.

By the end, I could say it with a straight face. Almost.

· · ·

The smoke alarm

The next morning, I found Theo by the shelves. My hands were sweaty. I took a breath, and I said it exactly the way we had practiced.

“Thank you for asking. I can’t this time.”

I waited for the ceiling to fall. It did not. Theo blinked and said, “Oh. Okay! No worries, I’ll ask Marta.” And then he went back to his map as if nothing had happened at all, because for him, nothing had.

The guilt came anyway. It sat on my chest all through math like a small, stubborn cat. I did not argue with it. I told it, “I hear you. You’re a smoke alarm,” and I let it purr until it got bored.

That evening, I did the thing I would have lost on Saturday. I made tea, took one cookie, and spread a big sheet of paper on the kitchen table. I drew stars and hearts and silly squiggles with no reason at all, and I ate the cookie slowly, one bite at a time.

The guilt faded a little more with every star.

I still say yes to plenty of things, and I mean it when I do. But now, before I answer, I try to ask myself one question: if I say yes, what am I saying no to? Sometimes the honest answer is my own Sunday.

It turns out that a kind no is not the opposite of being kind. It is just a yes to something I needed, too.

With love,
Lina
