How I Stopped Checking My Phone 10 Times Before Sleep

Stories · 2026-10-25

It’s 11:40 PM, and I’ve checked my phone nine times.

Nothing on it has changed.

I know it’s nine because I count with hair ties. One moves from my left wrist to my right every time I pick the phone up. Like a very sad abacus. I started three weeks ago, as a joke, to prove I wasn’t really checking that much.

The joke has not gone the way I planned.

Did they see it?

Who saw it?

What if Ms. Alvarez saw it?

The message that wasn’t for them

Here’s what I did. Our history group chat has five students and, since last Thursday, Ms. Alvarez, who was added “to see our progress.”

At 8:15, Marcus asked who was making the timeline slides. My thumbs answered before my brain could stop them:

“Sure, I’ll just do all of it. Again.”

It was meant for Zoe, in our other thread, the one where we complain about everything. I opened the wrong one.

I deleted it in twenty seconds. That sounds like a rescue until you see what’s left behind: a gray line that says This message was deleted. It’s like erasing a sentence from a chalkboard and leaving a perfectly clean rectangle. Everyone knows something was there.

Check number ten

At 11:52, I checked again. “Read by 4.”

No replies. Four people had seen the gray rectangle, four people had said nothing, and my brain turned that silence into a full trial. Jury. Judge. A very disappointed bailiff.

So I did the responsible thing. I put the phone on the dresser, all the way across the room.

At 12:10, I walked over there “to check the time.”

The time was on my phone. That’s the whole problem. My left wrist was bare by then, so I counted the rest on my fingers, which isn’t a system. It’s a confession.

· · ·

The question the screen couldn’t answer

Around 12:30, lying in the dark with the phone glowing across the room like a tiny, judgmental nightlight, I noticed something.

The screen could tell me who had read the message. It could never tell me what any of them thought.

For that, I’d have to ask an actual person. Terrible idea. Also the only one on offer.

I didn’t text Zoe. She’d been up at dawn all week, and I wasn’t going to drop one more thing into her phone at midnight. I’d say it out loud.

Then I fell asleep the way a phone dies at one percent: no warning, still holding on.

Eucalyptus and a half-eaten bagel

Zoe showed up at the bus stop with a pencil tucked in her curls, a tiny gold star pinned to her cardigan, and a smell of eucalyptus and wet cardboard. She’s been working mornings at her aunt’s flower shop since the holiday rush, and she carries it around like a very green rumor.

“I did something dumb,” I said, before I could rehearse it. I told her everything: the message, the wrong thread, the gray rectangle.

Zoe stopped chewing. “I muted that chat on Sunday. I haven’t read a single thing.”

Relief started climbing up my spine. Then she frowned.

“Wait. ‘Again’?”

“It was a joke.”

“About me?”

“About the slides. About… okay, a little about you.”

“I did the maps for the last project, Lina. By myself. At my kitchen table.” She said it flatly, not loud, and that was worse. “And if you were drowning, you could’ve just asked me. I’d have said yes.”

“I didn’t want to add to your pile,” I said. “You’ve been so tired.”

“Deciding I’m too busy isn’t the same as asking me.” She let that sit for a second, then sighed. “But thank you for telling me. I’m still a little mad.”

“That’s fair.”

“It’s very fair.” Her curls were sliding out of their clip. “Mine snapped in the flower cooler. Can I borrow one?”

She was already tugging a blue hair tie off my right wrist. She twisted her curls into a puff and looked at the bare spot on my arm. “Does this mess up your count?”

“Honestly? I think it helps.”

· · ·

What the chat said (and didn’t)

Before first period, I wrote to the whole group: That deleted message was sarcasm meant for someone else, and it wasn’t fair to any of you. I’ll make the timeline slides if someone can take sources.

My thumb hovered over Send for an embarrassingly long time.

Marcus replied in under a minute with a thumbs-up and “tbh I thought it was funny.” Priya sent a heart. Tessa said nothing. Ms. Alvarez said nothing either, but at the end of class she paused at my desk and said, “Looking forward to the timeline,” in a voice so ordinary I almost didn’t know what to do with it.

Tessa stayed quiet all day. I don’t know what that silence meant. Maybe nothing. Maybe she just doesn’t answer group chats.

By fifth period, I was doing math. Tessa had seen my apology before first period. It was now almost two. That was nearly six hours of silence, which my brain converted, with great confidence, into a verdict.

I stared at the back of her head for most of the lesson. She was drawing a lopsided cactus in her margin, and at one point she laughed at something Marcus whispered, a small, ordinary laugh that had nothing to do with me.

Or maybe it did. That was the hard part. A person can laugh and still be quietly upset, or laugh and be completely fine, and no amount of staring would tell me which. Only asking would. I wasn’t brave enough for that today.

So I wrote talk to Tessa? on the back of my hand, in pen. It looked like a very small, very honest reminder.

At lunch, I checked once, moved one hair tie, and put the phone in my bag. The afternoon went on without a verdict.

That night, I checked three times. I’m not going to pretend it was zero.

Three hair ties on my right wrist. One in Zoe’s curls. And my phone charging in the dark, doing nothing at all.

With love,
Lina
