How I Stopped Thinking My Friends Were Mad at Me

Stories · 2026-10-29

Sara answered my message with one letter.

I had sent her three sentences, a drawing of a lopsided cat and a heart. It had taken me twenty minutes to make the cat look friendly. She wrote back: k.

No heart. No cat. Not even a period, which somehow made it worse. I looked at that single letter for so long that my phone dimmed itself, politely, as if to give me some privacy.

Then I did what I always do. I started to investigate.

Sara usually sent me three exclamation marks. Sara had not looked up in the hallway that morning. Sara had sat at the far end of the lunch table, holding her tray like a shield. Put together, those facts could only mean one thing: I had done something terrible, and everybody knew except me.

I could not name the terrible thing. That did not slow me down at all. By the time the bell rang, I had built a whole courtroom in my head, and I was the defendant, the judge and the very strict jury.

What did I say wrong?

Is she angry at me?

Does everyone know except me?

The mind is a very bad detective

I told Zoe at the bike racks. I spoke quickly, in one long breath, with the evidence arranged in order.

She did not say, “I’m sure it’s nothing.” She never says that. It sounds kind, but it only makes your brain stand up and argue.

Instead, she asked, “What do you know, and what are you guessing?”

I opened my mouth to say that I knew everything. Then I closed it and thought about it properly.

“I know she wrote k,” I said slowly. “I know she didn’t wave.”

“And the rest?”

“…The rest is a movie I made.”

“Exactly.” Zoe tapped the handlebar. “Your brain is a detective who solves every case with the same answer: it was you. That’s not detective work, Lina. That’s a habit with a magnifying glass.”

I laughed, a little shakily, and something in my chest let go by one notch.

The Maybe Jar

That evening, Zoe came over with an empty glass jar and a pad of tiny paper slips. She put them down on my bedroom floor like a doctor setting out instruments.

“New rule,” she said. “For every mad-at-me story, we write three boring reasons. The more boring, the better. Nobody gets to be the villain.”

We sat cross-legged on the rug and wrote them together. Her phone was at 2%. She was hurrying to the dentist. She had a headache and a test. I wrote that she might have been staring at her tray because she was hungry. Zoe wrote that perhaps Sara’s cat had eaten a sock.

It was surprising how easily the reasons came once I stopped trying to be guilty. Each one was dull and ordinary and entirely believable, like a sock-eating cat.

I folded each slip in half and dropped it into the jar. When I shook it, it rattled, a small, plain, reasonable sound. It was the opposite of a courtroom.

Ask once, then wait

“And now,” said Zoe, “the brave part. You don’t guess anymore. You ask. Once.”

We wrote the message together, short and gentle: Hey, I noticed you were quiet today. No pressure, but are we okay? I wanted to add three paragraphs of apology. Zoe held the phone away from me until I agreed to leave it short.

My thumb hovered over send like a bird deciding whether to land. Then I pressed it, turned the phone face down, and went to make tea. That was the second brave part, and honestly the harder one: waiting without checking.

I checked eleven times. I counted. But I checked with the tea in my hand, and that felt like a small victory.

Sara wrote back the next morning, before breakfast.

“OMG I’m so sorry!! I had a migraine all week and my phone was at 2%. I didn’t even see you in the hall, I was squinting at everything. Are WE okay?? Also your cat drawing is the best thing that happened to me all week.”

Underneath, she had added four hearts, a cat and one very small, perfect period.

I laughed until my eyes were wet. The migraine had been in the jar, almost word for word. It was sitting on my shelf, being boring.

What I keep in the jar

I still keep the jar on my shelf. I won’t tell you my brain stopped building courtrooms, because it didn’t. It builds one almost every week, with great enthusiasm and a tiny gavel.

But now I have a routine. I write three boring reasons. I separate what I know from what I guess. I ask once, kindly, and then I wait with my tea.

Most of the time, the answer is a headache, a dead phone, or a tray held too tightly. Sometimes the answer is something real, and we talk about it, and that is fine too. A real problem I can talk about is so much smaller than a courtroom I have to defend all by myself.

My friends were not mad at me. They were just having weeks of their own.

With love,
Lina
